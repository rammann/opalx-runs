How the comparison works

Each case isolates one magnet. The same particle file is fed to both programs, so they cannot be given different beams. Both read the same .g4blmap file — nothing is converted. Three things are compared.

The field, with no tracking. Both programs are asked for the field at the same points. A difference here means the file is being read differently, or the magnet is in a different place.

Nineteen particles. One on the reference path, then a small step either way in each of the six coordinates, then a wide step either way in x, y and momentum. The small steps give a transfer matrix; the wide ones show where the map stops behaving linearly.

Twenty thousand particles. A Gaussian bunch, 10 mm across in x and 3 mm in y, matched particle for particle between the two programs, so the comparison stays per particle rather than only comparing beam sizes.

What limits the comparison

G4beamline writes six digits. When a particle is 1.27 m off centre that is steps of 10 µm, and nothing smaller can be seen. The cases at the real muE4 angle keep the beam within 35 mm of the reference line, so there the limit is 0.1 µm — about a hundred times finer. Every "allowed" figure in the tables below is derived from that limit, not chosen to make a result pass.

Spin

Both codes integrate the Thomas-BMT equation and neither had ever been checked against the other. In OPALX it is switched on by one attribute, POLARIZATION on the BEAM, and no input file in the repository used it — only unit tests, which deliberately do not check which way the spin turns.

A uniform Bz, where the answer is a formula

A muon travelling along a uniform field feels no force at all, so the orbit is a straight line and the only thing that changes is the spin. It turns about the field by (1 + G) times the cyclotron angle. One metre of 0.0933979467 T is exactly one radian for a 28 MeV/c muon.

Why OPALX sits further from the formula, and why that is not the equation

OPALX is a hundred times further from the closed form than G4beamline. Shrinking the time step shows what that is: every error sits inside half a step's worth of rotation, and that bound falls with the step, but the error jumps about inside it rather than falling smoothly.

Geant4 steps exactly to a geometry boundary; OPALX steps straight through one and carries up to half a step of extra rotation.

The anomaly itself

In a field across the direction of travel the spin turns (1 + Gγ) times faster than the momentum, so after a bend of θ the spin leads the momentum by Gγθ. That is the only term in the equation which is not the same one that turns the momentum. Holding the bend angle fixed and changing the momentum makes that angle a straight line in γ whose slope is G, so the five cases together measure the anomaly instead of comparing two numbers once.

The whole muE4 line

The same 22 field maps and 22 recording planes as the section below, with spin on in both codes and the spin starting along the direction of travel.

OPALX reports the spin in a different frame from the momentum. A MONITOR records position and momentum in its own frame — Monitor::apply runs after the bunch has been transformed into that frame, which is why the plane-crossing test is on R(2) — but it takes the spin straight from pc->Pol, and ParticleContainer::transformBunch rotates R, P, E and B while leaving Pol alone. G4beamline rotates both into the centreline frame. For a plane that is not turned the two frames are the same, which is why none of the uniform-field cases above could show it; muE4's planes are turned by up to 108 degrees, so it shows there. Turning OPALX's spin into each plane's own frame by that plane's THETA makes the two codes agree.

The difference is not merely large, it is exactly what a frame rotation gives. Turning a unit vector by an angle t moves it by 2 sin(t/2):

Turning OPALX's spin into each plane's own frame removes almost all of it — the median falls from 0.68 to between 0.001 and 0.012. What is left is a real difference, and it is the size the field edges predict: the uniform Bz case showed OPALX carrying up to half a step of extra spin rotation at each edge, this line has 22 maps and so 44 edges, and 44 of those with random signs comes to roughly 2e-4 rad.

This is not a precise measurement of the anomaly and was never going to be. The signal is under a milliradian and the accumulated edge error is a few tenths of one, so they are comparable. The anomaly is measured by the uniform-field scan above, to 8 parts per million. What this line shows is that spin survives 22 real field maps and 108° of bending with nothing going wrong. Note also that the naive Gγθ = 2.27 mrad does not apply here: the WSX solenoid turns the spin about the direction of travel before the beam reaches any bend, so the spin is no longer along the momentum when the bending starts.

One more thing this run shows. The length of the spin vector must stay at 1, and in OPALX it drifts to 1.0000232 over the line while G4beamline holds 1.0000000. The rotation itself conserves the length exactly; the storage does not — the spin is kept as three floats rather than doubles (ParticleContainer.hpp:124), and about 250 000 steps of rounding back to float accumulate to roughly that.

What this cannot cover

FROMFILE ignores any spin in the particle file and stamps the one BEAM vector on every particle, so a beam whose particles start with different spins cannot be compared — which is exactly what the real muE4 surface muon file has. POLARIZATION is also rejected for anything but muons, even though the electron and proton anomalies are present in the source. Neither code applies the force from the magnetic moment: G4beamline documents that as a Geant4 bug and the OPALX pusher only turns the spin, so on that point they agree.

A halo the real line would never have

Taken at face value the beam size in y at the last plane is 4.35 % apart (+815 µm), which looks bad. It is not the beam. About 20 particles out of 10 000 fly to y = ±300 mm, far outside every field map — the ASR61 maps reach ±120 mm vertically and the ASR62 maps ±110 mm. Out there neither code has any field, and whether a particle was inside or outside at the boundary differs, so those few part company completely; the worst is 645 mm apart.

Those particles exist only because the collimators were opened for this run. The real line scrapes them long before. Counting only what fits through the tightest collimator muE4 has — 190 mm across, 105 mm high — the same number becomes -0.2 µm, and the beam size in x is 0.036 % apart.

Opening the collimators isolated the optics as intended, but it produced a halo with no physical meaning that then dominated the beam size. Both sets of numbers are given so it is clear which is which.

What is still open

The step size has not been shown to be small enough on the full line. The single magnet cases showed that only halving the step and looking can answer that, and that has not been done here. The worst-case differences of 100 to 200 µm in the middle of the line could be step size or could be real.

What this does not cover

One map file is missing. muE4 uses six; these cases cover five. The solenoid file wsx_total.g4blmap is the only one in the older, round format, and it is the only file OPALX reads with different code. Its only comparison is a pair of older notebooks that run at step sizes this report shows are too large, and that compare the field only along the axis. Its off-axis field has never been compared with anything.

Two cases have not settled. ASR61 group at the real muE4 angle still shows a difference that shrinks every time the step size is halved, and the shrinking has not stopped. Whether any of what is left is a real difference between the programs is not yet answered, and four of its checks are left failing rather than given a looser limit.

Nothing physical beyond the magnet. No material, no scattering, no decay, no space charge, no apertures. One particle type, one charge, one momentum. The beam is 10 mm across where muE4's pipe is 400 mm, so only the middle of each magnet is tested.

Inside the field, the angle is unreliable. Both programs place a recording plane's position correctly but report the momentum from whichever step the particle happened to be on. In a field the momentum is changing, so a recording plane sitting inside a magnet shows an angle difference of about one step — 0.26 mm worth on the ASR61 magnet. Planes outside the field do not have this problem.
